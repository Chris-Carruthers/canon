# Voice: One-pager

## Write it like

**Primary — the Amazon narrative one-pager.** Prose, one page, ends in a specific ask.

**Supporting — [product-spec.md](product-spec.md)** for the discipline about problem
statements; the same rule applies at a tenth the length.

## Why them

The one-pager's whole value is the **constraint**. One page is short enough that
writing it is not an investment, which is what lets somebody kill the idea without
anyone having lost an afternoon. The moment it becomes two pages that property is gone
and you have written a bad spec instead of a good one-pager.

Amazon's narrative form is the right model because it ends in an **ask**. Most
lightweight documents end in a summary, which leaves the reader with nothing to do and
the author waiting for a response that never comes. Naming the decision and the person
who owns it is what makes a page of prose into a rung on a ladder.

## Do not write it like

**A slide deck flattened into a document.** Bullets, a heading per idea, no connective
sentences, and an ambient assumption that somebody will present it.

The failure is that bullets let you skip the reasoning between points, and at this
length there is nowhere else for the reasoning to live. A one-pager is *only* argument
— it has no design section, no criteria, no appendix. Strip the connective tissue and
nothing is left but assertions, which the reader has no way to evaluate and will
therefore either wave through or reject on instinct.

The second failure is **the summary ending**: closing with "next steps: discuss." That
is a document asking to be a meeting, and it will get one, and the meeting will
re-derive everything the page was supposed to settle.

## The moves

**One page, and mean it.** If it does not fit, the idea is not clear enough to ask
anybody about yet. Cut, do not spill.

**Prose, not bullets.** At this length the connective sentences *are* the content.

**End with the specific decision you want, and from whom.** "Feedback" is not a
decision. "Can I spend two weeks on this in September, yes or no" is.

**Include why it might be wrong.** One paragraph, honestly. A one-pager with no
counter-case reads as advocacy and gets discounted as advocacy.

**Cost as a range, with what it trades against.** "About two weeks, which is the
connector work" tells a reader more than any single number.

**Delete a section rather than writing TBD.** An absent section is a signal; "TBD"
reads like content and is not.

## Go read

- Colin Bryar & Bill Carr, *Working Backwards* — the narrative chapters
- Jeff Bezos, 2004 internal note on banning PowerPoint — the reasoning is the useful part
- Any Amazon shareholder letter, for the register — <https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news>

## Before saving

1. Does it actually fit on one page?
2. Is it prose, with the reasoning between the points written down?
3. Does it end in a named decision from a named person?
4. Is there an honest paragraph on why this might be wrong?
5. Is every section either filled in or deleted — with no "TBD" anywhere?
