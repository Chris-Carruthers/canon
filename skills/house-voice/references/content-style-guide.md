# Voice: Content & UX Copy Guide

## Write it like

**Primary — the GOV.UK content style guide** and the GOV.UK service manual on writing
for users.

**Supporting — Nicole Fenton & Kate Kiefer Lee**, *Nicely Said*, and **Sarah Richards**,
*Content Design*.

## Why them

GOV.UK's guide is the world standard for functional interface writing, and the reason
is the same one that makes their design system good: it was written for a readership
that **cannot be trained and cannot be argued with**. Thousands of writers across
hundreds of organisations, none of whom can be brought into a room. That forces every
rule to be checkable by somebody with no context — so the guide is almost entirely
pairs of strings and mechanical rules, and almost entirely free of adjectives.

It is also the best model for **evidence**. Where GOV.UK says use this word rather than
that one, there is usually user research behind it, and where there is not, the entry
says so. A style guide that cannot say why loses every argument with a confident
stakeholder.

Richards adds the reframe that content is a design material with the same obligations
as any other, and Fenton & Lee give the register for the human moments — errors,
empty states, confirmations — without sliding into whimsy.

## Do not write it like

**A brand adjective list.** "Our voice is friendly, human, clear and confident."

This describes every product ever shipped. It gives a writer nothing to act on, it
cannot fail a string in review, and — worst — it *feels* like a style guide, so it
occupies the slot where a real one would go and nobody writes the real one. Ask what a
reviewer does with it: nothing. There is no string it rejects.

The replacement is mechanical: **five before/after pairs beat any number of adjectives.**
Show the string you want and the string you do not, and give the reason.

The second failure is **omitting error messages** — the highest-value section and the
most commonly missing. They are read at the worst moment by the most frustrated reader,
and generic guidance does the most damage exactly there.

## The moves

**Every rule ships with a counter-example.** ✅ and ❌, real strings. A rule without one
is unenforceable, because two people will read it as endorsing opposite strings and
both will be sincere.

**Write the error-message section first.** What happened, why, and what to do next — in
that order, in the reader's terms, without blaming them.

**Keep the rule list short.** Only rules people actually break. A long list is ignored
entirely, which is worse than a short one being incomplete.

**Do the boring mechanical rules properly.** Capitalisation, dates, numbers, currency,
the Oxford comma. Dull, and the reason reviews stop being about commas.

**Ban the internal term explicitly.** Every product has a word that means something
inside the company and nothing outside it. Name it in the terminology table.

**Link text must make sense read alone.** Never "click here" — a screen reader may
announce links out of context, and that is a correctness rule rather than a style one.

## Before saving

1. Does every rule ship with a ❌ counter-example as well as a ✅?
2. Is there an error-message section, with what happened / why / what next?
3. Are there any bare adjectives standing in for a rule?
4. Are the mechanical rules — dates, numbers, capitalisation — actually decided?
5. Would every link text make sense read out of context?
