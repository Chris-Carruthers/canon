# Voice: Vision and strategy narrative

For `Vision/` notes and strategy documents in `Outputs/` — the company- or
product-level memo that changes what people work on. **Not** a spec. If the document
has acceptance criteria in it, you want [product-spec.md](product-spec.md) instead.

## Write it like

**Primary — Satya Nadella**, "Bold Ambition & Our Core" (2014) and the annual
shareholder letters.

**Supporting — Fidji Simo**, the Instacart S-1 CEO letter and her essays on operating,
for a strategy that commits to something specific; and **Ravi Mehta's** Product Strategy
Stack for the skeleton underneath.

## Why them

Nadella's 2014 memo is the studyable example of the genre's defining move: **the
reframe.** It does not argue that Microsoft should try harder at what it was doing. It
restates what business the company is in, and every subsequent priority follows from
the restatement rather than being asserted alongside it. That is what separates a
strategy narrative from a plan — a plan lists what you will do, a narrative changes what
counts as a good idea.

The shareholder letters are the better prose model. They do the harder thing of naming
what did not work, in public, with a number attached, before describing what is next.
A strategy document that only contains the new direction reads as enthusiasm; one that
prices the last direction reads as judgement.

Be honest about the limits. The 2014 memo was also widely criticised for its length and
abstraction, and the criticism was fair. Steal the reframe; do not steal the word count.

Simo is the corrective. The Instacart S-1 letter commits — to a specific customer, a
specific bet, in language a reader could later hold her to. Her operating essays do the
same at team scale. Where Nadella gives you the altitude, Simo gives you the thing most
strategy documents lack, which is a sentence somebody could disagree with.

Mehta's Product Strategy Stack is not prose to imitate but structure to borrow:
mission → strategy → roadmap → goals, each level accountable to the one above. Most
strategy documents are actually a roadmap with a mission stapled on, and the stack is
the fastest way to find out that is what you have written.

## Sinek's WHY, and where it belongs

**This is the one place the Golden Circle earns its keep.** A vision document's opening
job is to make somebody care before you make them decide, and start-with-why is a
genuinely effective structure for that paragraph. Use it for the frame.

Then stop. The two failure modes are letting it run the whole document — where it
produces assent with nothing to act on — and letting it leak downstream into a spec,
where a stirring opening replaces the falsifiable problem statement. Vision persuades;
specs commit. Sinek is only good at the first, and the reference file for the second
names him as an anti-model for exactly this reason.

## Do not write it like

**A Sundar Pichai shareholder letter or Keyword post.** This is careful, warm,
well-constructed corporate prose, and it is engineered **not to create news**. Every
sentence is survivable in isolation; nothing in it can be wrong.

That is a legitimate objective for a public company communication and a disqualifying
one for an internal strategy memo. A strategy document exists to make some work
obviously correct and other work obviously not, and prose optimised against risk cannot
do that — it commits to nothing, so nothing follows from it, so nobody changes what they
were already doing. The tell is that you can delete any paragraph without changing the
meaning.

**Ivan Zhao** is worth naming separately, because the instinct to reach for him is
right. Notion's product principles and his interviews carry real taste — particularly
the insistence on fewer, more general primitives over more features. But the written
corpus is thin, so as a *prose* model it lands in the same trap as Ive: the model
supplies the idea of him rather than the substance. Use him as a design constraint
("could this be one primitive instead of three?"), not as a voice.

## The moves

**Lead with the reframe, not the list.** What business are we actually in; what has
changed about the world. The priorities are a consequence, and they read as arbitrary
if they arrive first.

**Price the last direction before selling the new one.** What we tried, what it cost,
what we learned. A strategy with no post-mortem in it is a wish.

**One sentence somebody could disagree with.** If every sentence is survivable, you have
written a Pichai letter. Find the claim that would make a smart colleague push back, and
make sure it is in there, stated plainly.

**Say what you are giving up.** A strategy is a set of things you have decided not to
do; the ones you are doing are just the residue. If nothing is being stopped, nothing
has been decided.

**Altitude discipline — one level, held.** Mission, strategy, roadmap and goals are four
documents. A vision note that drops into sprint detail has lost the authority that made
the altitude worth having.

**Numbers where they exist, and named absence where they do not.** "We do not have a
number for this yet" is honest and usable. An adjective standing in for a number is
neither.

## Before saving

1. Does the document open with a reframe, rather than with a list of priorities?
2. Is there at least one sentence a smart colleague could disagree with?
3. Does it say what is being **stopped**, not only what is starting?
4. Is the previous direction priced — what it cost, what was learned?
5. Does it stay at one altitude, without dropping into implementation detail?
