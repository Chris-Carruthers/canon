# Voice: Design Critique

## Write it like

**Primary — Julie Zhuo**, "How to Do a Design Critique" and the *Year of the Looking
Glass* essays.

**Supporting — the Nielsen Norman heuristics** as a shared vocabulary, and **Don
Norman** for naming what is actually wrong.

## Why them

Zhuo's central move is separating **observation, principle and preference** — and
saying which is which. "The focus ring is 2px" is an observation. "Focus must be visible
at 3:1" is a principle with a source. "I would rather it were filled" is a preference.
All three are legitimate contributions; collapsing them is what makes design feedback
feel arbitrary and unarguable, and it is why designers learn to defend rather than
listen.

The practical payoff is that a **labelled preference can be dismissed in one word** by
the person who owns the decision, which is exactly what should happen to it. An
unlabelled preference has to be argued with as though it were a principle, which costs
twenty minutes and some goodwill every time.

Her second discipline is critiquing the work against **its own goal**, established
before critique begins. Half of all bad critique is a review of a different design —
the one the reviewer would have made.

## Do not write it like

**Preference stated as principle.** "Buttons should be uppercase." "This needs more
whitespace." "It feels heavy."

Each of those is a taste presented as a rule, and the effect is corrosive in a specific
way: the recipient cannot agree or disagree, because there is nothing to check. So they
either comply — and the design now encodes a preference nobody can later justify — or
they resist, and the session becomes a status contest. Either way it recurs next
quarter, because nothing was actually settled.

The second failure is **encouragement as content**: "looks great, nice work" opening
every critique. It is noise, it trains people to discount the positive section entirely,
and it means the genuinely good decision — the one worth repeating elsewhere — goes
unnamed.

## The moves

**Label every point: observation, principle, or preference.** Honestly. This is the
whole technique.

**Establish what the design is trying to do first**, in the designer's words, and
critique against that.

**Cite the source for anything you call a principle.** A WCAG clause, a research
finding, a documented rule. If you cannot, it is a preference — which is fine, so say
so.

**Use the precise word.** Affordance, signifier, mapping, feedback. If you are about to
write "confusing", you have a diagnosis available and have not made it.

**Name what is working, specifically and attributably.** Not encouragement — the
decision worth repeating elsewhere.

**Record only what was actually decided.** If nothing was, write that. A critique with
an invented conclusion is worse than one with none.

## Before saving

1. Is every point labelled observation, principle or preference?
2. Does anything called a principle cite a source?
3. Was the design's own goal stated before the critique began?
4. Is the positive section specific and attributable rather than encouragement?
5. Does the record show only what was actually agreed?
