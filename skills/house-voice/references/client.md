# Voice: Client

## Write it like

**Primary — Erika Hall**, *Just Enough Research*. Findings stated at the confidence they
were actually obtained at, and no higher.

## Why them

Hall's central discipline is the one a client note most needs: **separating what you
observed from what you concluded.** A client note is written from partial evidence —
three calls, a procurement document, one frustrated email — and the temptation is to
smooth that into a confident characterisation, because a confident note is more useful
to read. It is also how a single bad meeting becomes a permanent institutional belief
about a customer.

She is also the right model for scope. *Just Enough* is the operative phrase: the note
should carry the context that changes how you build, and nothing that belongs in the CRM.
Deal mechanics, contact history and pipeline stage have a system of record; duplicating
them here creates a second source of truth that is always staler.

## Do not write it like

**A CRM note.** The register where sentiment is recorded as fact and attributed to an
organisation: "they are risk-averse", "they are unhappy with the integration", "they
want to move fast".

Three problems, all of them practical. It attributes a mood to a company, when the
actual fact was one person's position in one conversation. It carries no date, so a
statement true in March governs a decision in November. And it is unfalsifiable — nobody
can check "they are risk-averse", so it never gets revised, only compounded.

The fix is mechanical: name who said it, roughly when, and what they were reacting to.
"Their security lead pushed back on data residency in the March review" is the same
length and is actually usable.

## The moves

**Attribute and date every characterisation.** Who said it, roughly when. A claim about
a client with no source is a rumour with good posture.

**Separate observation from inference, visibly.** "They asked twice about data
residency" is an observation. "They are unlikely to accept a US-only region" is an
inference from it. Both are worth writing; conflating them is how the inference becomes
unchallengeable.

**Keep the constraints that change how we build.** Their EMR, their scale, their
regulatory position, their tolerance for change. This is the section that earns the
note. The things a new engineer would get wrong by assuming this client is like the
others.

**Leave deal mechanics in the CRM.** If the fact has a system of record elsewhere, link
or omit. Duplication here loses.

**Write the commitments that still bind us.** Past promises and escalations that
constrain what we can now do — linked to the decision that made them, not restated.

**Nothing here that you could not defend showing them.** This is a git repository and
history is permanent. That constraint improves the writing.

## Go read

- Erika Hall, *Just Enough Research* — and her writing at <https://mule.design/>
- Steve Portigal, *Interviewing Users* — on recording what was said rather than what you took from it

## Before saving

1. Does every characterisation carry who said it and roughly when?
2. Is observation visibly separated from inference?
3. Does the note duplicate anything the CRM already owns?
4. Would you be comfortable if the client read this note?
5. Is there anything here that is a mood rather than a fact?
